Dancing Elements

Reflections on dancing and its innate human nature

The school gymnasium was humbly decorated with streamers, technicolored lights, and teenage insecurities. Teacher chaperones watched from the side, while uncomfortable thirteen-year-olds stood in the center. A KidsBop soundtrack played over sparsely placed speakers while a few class clowns managed to gain some laughs by throwing exaggerated whips and nae naes. And there I was, in a cesspool of peak adolescence and angst. The quintessential 8th-grade dance, where the risk of embarrassment was high, and where I found a new fascination in my shoelaces. The rest of the night played out in the same awkward manner as I made myself comfortable in a corner. The dance eventually came to an anti-climatic end where the only shuffling done was by the students attempting to leave the gymnasium.

The air was temperate and pleasant. The sun was beginning to set, the sky was streaked with pinks and blues. It was the perfect summer day, and I decided to spend it at a free concert in the park. The stage and dance floor were cocooned with grass where scattered picnic blankets and groups of people were lounging idly. As everybody found their place among the dance floor and friends, the buzz of conversation and laughter served as a sad reminder that I was alone. I felt a deep desire to disappear, but I was desperate to prove something to myself. Fresh from a heartbreak, I needed to declare a sense of assuredness in my solitude. But, standing there, amid 30-something-year-olds holding overpriced beer, I found myself feeling how I did some years back in a poorly decorated gymnasium with an over-attentive fascination in my shoelaces. I heard them before I saw them. It was raucous, obnoxious cheering coming from the front of the stage. A group of electric people demanded space on the dance floor with their incessant head-banging. I watched them for some time, feeling jealous that I wasn’t there. Instead, I was swaying awkwardly in a crowd of people nearly double my age, hardly able to watch the band, too focused on being a voyeur of my embarrassment. That is until I realized, I wasn’t jealous; I was regretful. Without much thought, I was weaving my way through the crowd. I found myself at the front, tapping the shoulder of a thirty-something-year-old sweating from her dance. I had to shout when I asked her if I could join them. She eagerly nodded her head yes and made room for me at the front. Suddenly, I was no longer that awkward 8th grader who was too uncomfortable to make eye contact with anything but the ground. I was center front making eye contact with the guitarist. I felt a surge of energy propel me with every thump of the bass reverberating through the crowd and into my bones. The air became thick with exhilaration, and amidst the chaos and sweat, there was instinctive liberation. I had whiplash in the morning.

Fairy lights hung from the center of the ceiling and snaked towards the sides of the walls, veiling the room in warm light. The space was intimate and cozy. I had begun taking swing dancing classes at this venue, but rather than the typical jazz band at the front and groups of two twirling together, some indie rock band had taken center, and a small crowd gathered around them. There was a sizable gap between the crowd and the band. People were shying away from the front, like a falling tide. I was swaying off to the side with a friend when a woman adorned in a flowing white dress with hair to match, strode to the front. Upon first glance, it struck me that I was seeing the liveliest ghost I’d ever laid eyes on. She possessed an elegance, grace, and confidence that glided her through the crowd that I thought was reserved for the dead. But when she started dancing, she epitomized energy, light, and life. I was sure the dead and living alike would envy her. I joined her some time into the set after I garnered enough of the same bravery that I had a few weeks back from the solo summer concert. It didn’t take much before we were dancing together; it was an assortment of jumping and spinning and cheering. The same loose disregard for inhibitions took over as I moved with the music and the lively ghost. I spoke to her after the band had finished their set, and we had both caught our breaths. Her name was Mary Love. She had a vibrant voice and a gap between her two front teeth when she smiled. She told me about her life. In her twenties, she followed a punk band around, and her 40s were spent dancing across the country frequenting as many concerts as she could. She also told me about her death. How when she dies, she’ll go out dancing. She was timeless and more alive than anybody I’d ever met. Her gapped teeth and free spirit reminded me of the vitality of a flame. I remember thinking about flames – how this natural element dances and moves to the world around it. How flames transform: bright, fierce, and untameable. It struck me then how humans are a natural element too. Flames flicker, tides fall, Earth rotates, wind whistles, and humans dance. A conglomeration of elements in their natural form.

It was one of the first warm days, and flowers lining the arboretum were beginning to bud. The seed of spring was sprouting in Davis, California and the Saturday farmer’s market was in full swing. Jazzy tunes were playing as a live band stood at the front of the stalls. A mixture of college students and families were milling about. Some were shopping for fresh fruit, some were spectating the band, and one was dancing. I was among the spectating crowd. The dancer was a little girl. She was small and young. She couldn’t have been older than seven. She was moving her little arms and legs to the music, swaying when the saxophone had its solo, and switching directions when a trumpet joined. The crescendo of the song hit, and time slowed. Her head tilted and her eyes closed, the sun caressed her cheek. She threw her arms out and spun. At that moment I thought about the crucifixion of Christ. I have never been to church, but I imagined that if God did exist, a piece of him existed in this little girl. I started crying. I cried and I cried and I cried. I cried for the bumbling 13-year-old me who was too afraid to dance. I cried for the people who opened their dance circle to me. I cried for Mary Love and her stories. I cried for the innocence and the beauty of the little girl. I cried because I felt lucky to experience art and food and laughter. I cried because I felt lucky to cry. The song ended, the little girl ran to her parents, and I wiped away my last few tears.

Later that night, I left my dorm room to sit out in a field. There was a slight breeze and a whiff from the cow pen stationed next to my dorm came with it. The sky was clear of clouds but pimpled with stars. The tiny green soldiers of grass held dew on them, but I sat down anyway disregarding the pricks of water that soaked through my skirt. The moon loomed above, easing the stars in its slender and delicate crescent glow. I thought then about the moon’s command on rhythmic tides. I thought about stars and people and how elements of my body were formed in stardust. I thought about dancing and art and did you know there are 20,000-year-old cave paintings depicting dancers in Bonero? And did you know that Gods dance in every religion? And did you know that little girls dance without ever being taught? We have danced across galaxies and time, and there is evidence littered everywhere. That night, under the moon, under stardust, under everyone who came before me, I danced and I spun the way I was meant to do.
